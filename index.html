Backstory

Before this project, everything was manual.

At Unilever, the Distribution Trade team relied on Excel files for almost everything. People had to collect data from different sources, clean it by hand, and update reports every week. It worked — but it was slow, inconsistent, and hard to scale.

Sales and distribution teams depend on data to make daily decisions: performance tracking, stock movement, regional sales trends. But because the process was manual, insights often came too late — or required too much effort to prepare to be useful.

I saw this as a good opportunity to improve not just the reporting process, but also how quickly the team could make decisions.

An external vendor had previously been engaged to solve this, at approximately €60,000 per year. The solutions they built were technically competent but never truly fit the business — the communication gap between vendor and team was too wide. User adoption remained flat. The company decided to build the capability internally. I was recruited as part of that effort.

My Role

Analytics lead, from requirement to adoption.

I was the analytics lead on a team of three: my supervisor who provided business validation and deep distribution knowledge, myself handling all analytics and dashboard development, and an IT counterpart managing data pipelines and refresh automation.

My responsibilities spanned the full lifecycle — translating business requirements into technical solutions, validating data accuracy against manual benchmarks, building the dashboards in Power BI, and supporting user adoption through workshops and hands-on enablement sessions.

The Challenge

Thirteen months of building, learning, and iterating.

The project didn't follow a fixed script. It evolved — from a single intern trying to understand a business problem, to a contract-role developer owning a 12-dashboard reporting system used across the entire Distribution Trade team. Each phase taught something the previous one couldn't.

Month 1

Project Kickoff — Understanding the Problem

Joined as an intern. The focus was entirely on understanding the business — not building anything technical. Met stakeholders, mapped how different teams used Excel, and learned why the current process was failing. This grounding shaped every decision that followed.

Months 2–4

Learning & First Prototype

Started Power BI from scratch — no prior experience. While learning data modelling, DAX, and dashboard design, I simultaneously cleaned multiple Excel data sources and identified key KPIs with stakeholders. The first prototype included YTD sales, monthly targets, and MOM/YOY comparisons — with filters by Region, Distributor, Brand, SKU, and Unit Type. Static reports became interactive explorations.

Months 5–11

Expansion & Full System Development

After the prototype was validated and well-received, I transitioned from intern to contract role — gaining full ownership of the system. The single prototype expanded into approximately 12 dashboard templates covering key account performance, promotional sales analysis, stock monitoring, sales rep tracking, and regional breakdowns across West Malaysia, East Malaysia, Sabah, Sarawak, Brunei, and more. Highly iterative — user feedback drove every sprint.

Months 12–13

Launch, Training & Adoption

Official system launch across the Distribution Trade team. Ran training sessions and workshops, supported onboarding for teams transitioning from Excel, and created documentation for ongoing usage and maintenance. Addressed live user questions during early adoption — this phase determined whether 13 months of work actually changed anything.

Successful systems are not built in one attempt. They are built through continuous learning, feedback, and iteration over time.

What I Built

An automated platform, not just a dashboard.

Over 13 months, the system evolved from a basic prototype into a fully operational reporting platform covering 12 dashboards across regions and business themes. Regional access controls were implemented to ensure appropriate data visibility.

Dashboard Layer

KPI cards, MTD/QTD/YTD sales summaries, regional breakdowns, outlet activity tracking, brand-level performance, and competitive trend analysis.

Data Pipeline

Connected multiple data sources — SAP, SharePoint, Azure — via Power Query. Cleaned, restructured, and reshaped raw tables into a reliable reporting model.

DAX Measures

Built advanced calculations, dynamic measures, and iterative functions to power YOY, MOM, and target-vs-achievement comparisons across all dashboards.

Automation

Configured scheduled and incremental refresh via Power Automate. Daily discrepancy monitoring to catch upstream data anomalies before they reached users.

Technical Foundation

A relational data model built for scale.

The backbone of the platform was a carefully designed relational database connecting over ten tables — Primary Sales, Secondary Sales, DTKA Target, COTC Target, Calendar, Product Master, Master Distributor, and more. Each relationship was mapped deliberately to ensure accurate cross-filtering across every dashboard.

Data types spanned Date/Time, Integer/Decimal, and Text fields. Dashboard content covered Sales Summary (MTD, QTD, YTD), Sales Growth Comparison (YOY, YTD), and performance slicing by Business Units, Regions, Channels, Brands, and Product Names.

The Hard Part

Technology was easy. Changing habits was hard.

The primary user base — experienced sales managers and regional leads — had spent years building expertise in Excel. Switching to a new system wasn't just a tool change. For many of them, it felt like a challenge to something they'd already mastered.

"

Some team members were very used to Excel and were initially unsure about switching to a new system.

"

Different users had different expectations — some wanted simplicity, others wanted more detailed data.

"

Can I trust these numbers? How do I know this data is current?

To address this, every dashboard metric was validated against existing manual reports before launch — establishing a 99.9% accuracy target as the baseline for trust. Launch was structured as a conference, not a rollout: workshops, hands-on breakout sessions, and interactive demos so skeptical users could experience the efficiency gains themselves rather than being told about them.

I had to constantly balance both sides — simplicity for some, depth for others — while continuing to improve the product iteration by iteration.

Outcome

After several iterations, adoption changed everything.

The dashboard became widely used across the Distribution Trade team. Manual reporting workload dropped significantly. Managers gained on-demand access to updated performance data — without waiting for someone to compile a report.

More importantly, it changed the way the team interacted with data. Instead of spending time preparing reports, they could focus on understanding insights — and making faster decisions from them.

The platform also replaced the outsourced BI model entirely, eliminating approximately €60,000 in annual vendor spend while delivering a solution with far better adoption and business fit.

What I Learned

Good data products are about clarity, not complexity.

01

Understand people before understanding data

The most useful thing I did early in the project was not building — it was listening. Understanding what users actually needed versus what they asked for made every subsequent decision sharper.

02

Build → Feedback → Improve → Repeat

The first version was very basic. That was intentional. Getting something in front of users early, even imperfect, generated better feedback than any requirements document ever could.

03

Adoption is a product problem, not a training problem

If users aren't adopting a tool, the instinct is to train them harder. The real answer is usually to make the tool easier to trust. Validation and accuracy came before everything else.

This wasn't a dashboard project.

It was a change management project disguised as an analytics project. The real outcome wasn't 12 dashboards — it was a Distribution team that became more confident in its data, more consistent in how it made decisions, and more capable of asking the questions that drive competitive advantage.

Without waiting for someone to build a report first.
