1.

The real problem wasn't reporting. It was decision-making.

Unilever's Distribution team ran on Excel files, manual calculations, and periodic report cycles. Getting a straight answer to a business question required hours of gathering, cross-checking, and reconciling data — before any analysis could begin.

An external vendor had been engaged to solve this. Annual spend: approximately €60,000. Return on investment: low.

"By the time it reached decision-makers, the opportunity had already passed."

2.

Three problems hiding inside one problem.

The vendor's low ROI wasn't a technology failure. It was three compounding organizational failures — each masking the others.

Addressing only one would have left the others intact. All three required a coordinated response.

Exhibit 2

Three compounding gaps in the outsourced BI model.

Communication

Requirements lost in translation

Business users couldn't speak in technical requirements. Technical teams couldn't speak in distribution logic. Every change request degraded in transit.

Comprehension

Dashboards without domain knowledge

Promotional structures, regional hierarchies, category nuances — these take months to learn. The vendor never had that time.

Trust

Adoption blocked by unverifiable outputs

Even when reports existed, no one could audit them. Excel remained the default because it was controllable. The platform wasn't used.

3.

The mandate was analytics. The real job was trust.

The strategy wasn't to build better dashboards. It was to earn the right to replace the old ones.

That required sequencing the work carefully — listening before building, validating before launching, and treating the rollout as a change management event rather than a software release.

Exhibit 3

Three-phase approach to platform delivery and adoption.

Phase 1 — Listen before you build

The first step was not opening a data tool. It was sitting with sales managers, regional leads, and distribution heads to understand not what they reported, but what decisions they were actually trying to make. The gap between the two was significant.

Phase 2 — Build with validation at the center

Every metric was validated against existing manual reports before going live. A 99.9% accuracy target was set as the standard. Discrepancies — from promotional configurations, unit conversions, or transaction anomalies — were documented and escalated, never hidden.

Phase 3 — Launch as a change management event

Technology was the easy part. Changing behavior was the hard part. Launch was structured as a conference — workshops, hands-on sessions, interactive breakouts. Not demos. The goal was for skeptical users to experience efficiency gains themselves.

4.

The most capable solution can fail if nobody opens it.

Seasoned sales managers and regional leads had spent years building expertise in Excel. The dashboards weren't just a tool change — they were a challenge to what those users had mastered.

The turning point wasn't a feature. It was the moment a skeptical regional manager found an insight he couldn't have found in Excel — and acted on it before his peers knew the question had even been asked.

5.

Dashboards answered "what." Analysis answered "why."

Beyond operational reporting, a separate layer of market intelligence analysis was conducted using Nielsen data to answer harder questions about brand performance, category trends, and promotional effectiveness.

The goal was always to produce a recommendation — not just a report.

Exhibit 5

Analytical approach applied to a Dove shampoo promotional evaluation.

Reporting mode

What happened?

Dove shampoo sales volume increased 8% during the promotional period.

↳ Output: a number. No context. No action.

Analysis mode

What happened — and what should we do?

Dove +8% vs. historical baseline +3%, category growth +11%, primary competitor +15%.

↳ Promotion underperformed vs. category. Investigate distribution gaps before next cycle.

6.

Twelve dashboards. One cultural shift.

The quantitative outcomes tell part of the story. But the number that matters most doesn't appear in any dashboard: the number of managers who stopped opening Excel.

Exhibit 6

Impact summary across four dimensions.

Direct

Replaced a €60,000 annual outsourced BI model with internal capability that could adapt to changing requirements in real time.

Operational

Reduced dependence on manual reporting cycles. Managers gained on-demand access to performance data across regions and brands.

Organizational

Increased BI adoption among non-technical users who had previously relied exclusively on Excel. Improved data confidence organization-wide.

Strategic

Enabled faster identification of performance risks and opportunities. Improved competitive visibility through Nielsen market intelligence integration.

This wasn't a technology project.

It was a change management project disguised as an analytics project. The work required translating between business language and data language — in both directions. It required building trust faster than skepticism could grow.

The real outcome was a Distribution organization that became more confident in its data, more consistent in how it made decisions, and more capable of asking the questions that drive competitive advantage — without waiting days for a report to be built.
